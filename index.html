A thesis in robot locomotion

Fiammetta learns where to push

This is a four-legged robot I wrote the walking software for. It walks with a classical controller, but the most delicate decision in that controller, how hard each foot presses on the ground, is made by a small neural network.

The robot beside this text is not a recording. It is the same control program that runs on the real robot, compiled for your browser, standing in the physics simulator I used to test it.

Or read on. Each chapter below sets the stage for what it describes.

How to drive. The round stick moves the robot: up is forward, sideways is a sidestep. The bar beside it turns. Both stay where you leave them; double-tap either, or press space, to stop. Keys: W and S forward and back, A and D sidestep, Q and E or the arrow keys turn. A game controller works too. Drag the robot's body to shove it; drag anywhere else to look around, pinch or scroll to zoom. Pick up does what an operator would: lift the robot, carry it, and set it down where you tap, facing the way you drag.

1 · The robot

Twenty-six kilograms, twelve motors, no eyes

Fiammetta Mk-II weighs 26.4 kg. Each leg has three joints: one swings the leg sideways at the hip, one swings the thigh, one bends the knee. Every joint has a RobStride RS03 motor that can produce 60 N·m for a moment but is rated for 20 N·m continuously.

It feels the world only through its own body: the angle and torque of each joint, and an inertial sensor in the trunk that measures rotation and acceleration. There is no camera and no laser scanner. When it climbs stairs, it finds each step with its feet.

So the robot never knows its own speed directly. A filter estimates it from the legs and the inertial sensor, and the controller uses only that estimate. In the strip at the bottom of the stage, the blue line is the estimate and the orange line is the simulator's true speed, which the controller never sees.

2 · The decision

Every 2 ms, each foot on the ground has to push with some force

The orange arrows at the feet are those forces. Shove the robot sideways and watch them lean against you. Together they decide whether the trunk stays level, keeps its height, and moves at the speed you asked for.

They cannot point anywhere. A foot that pushes too far sideways slips, so each force has to stay inside a cone set by friction, and the motors have limits of their own. When an arrow turns amber, the controller has run out of grip and is pulling the force back into the cone.

The bars beside the speed line show which feet are scheduled to be down (pale) and which ones the controller believes are touching the ground (dark). In a trot, the diagonal pairs alternate.

3 · The idea

Keep the rules. Learn only the forces.

There are two common ways to make a legged robot walk. One writes down the physics and solves for the forces, usually with a planner that looks a fraction of a second ahead. It is predictable, but its model of the robot is never quite right. The other trains a neural network to command the joints directly, by trial and error in simulation. It copes well with messy ground, but it needs a great deal of practice and is hard to reason about.

I kept the structure of the first and replaced one part of it. A gait clock still decides which feet are down. Simple rules still place the feet and lift them. A whole-body controller still turns forces into motor commands, five hundred times a second. Only the forces come from a network, fifty times a second, and its outputs are shaped so they always fall inside the friction cone.

Two copies of the robot now get the same command: forward at 0.5 m/s while turning. The one marked blue takes its forces from the hand-written rule, the one marked orange from the network. Same ground, same sensors, same motors. The panel at the top of the stage keeps score.

Over the full test, 107 situations and three separately trained networks, the learned forces cut the typical turn-rate error from 0.20 to 0.12 rad/s and raised the share of runs without a fall from 91 % to 94.5 %. A network that commands the joints directly, trained for as long, followed the commanded speed three times worse.

4 · Learning

Teaching it through a simpler world

The usual way to train such a network, PPO, tries small random variations and keeps the ones that score better. It works, but it learns from noise, so it needs many tries.

I used a method called SHAC, which follows the slope of the score directly: if this foot had pushed a little harder, how much better would the walk have been? Answering that needs derivatives of the physics. Full contact physics gives poor ones, because a foot touching down is an abrupt event. So the derivative comes from a much simpler model, the trunk as one rigid block pushed by four forces, while every value still comes from the full simulator.

I also checked whether the derivative through the full simulator could be used instead. On flat ground and gentle ramps it could. On steep ramps, blocks and stairs it often pointed the wrong way. The simple model was the better teacher for this robot.

Neither method wins everything. With the same controller around them, the SHAC network follows turns more closely and survives stairs more often. The PPO network follows forward speed slightly more closely and uses a little less torque.

5 · Ground

Ramps, blocks and stairs, climbed blind

The test ground is a grid of 16 m patches: flat floor, ramps of 5° to 20°, fields of random blocks up to 8 cm high, and stairs with 5 cm and 10 cm steps. The Ground button on the stage puts the robot on any of them.

On the 20° ramp, the rules alone fall in about one run in seven; with learned forces the robot almost never falls there. On 10 cm stairs going up, the network that commands the joints survives most often, but mostly because it hardly moves.

6 · Against a planner

Is learning worth it against a well-tuned planner?

The fair opponent is the part the network replaced: a model-predictive controller that solves an optimisation problem to plan the forces. I tuned one over many settings and put it in the same stack.

The result was close. In how often the robot stays up and how well it follows speed, the two cannot be told apart, and the planner uses slightly less joint torque. The difference is cost. The network takes about 15 µs to evaluate. The planner needs up to 2.2 ms, longer than one 2 ms control cycle, so it can replan only every 4 ms. At that rate the learned forces follow speed and turns more closely.

The score panel shows how long each controller takes per cycle, measured in your browser as it runs. The planner's number is the one to watch.

7 · Safety

A robot that knows when to give up

A walking robot that falls with its motors at full power can break itself. Around the walking controller sits a supervisor: a small state machine with limits on every command, checks on every sensor, and a learned detector that recognises a fall while it is happening. When it sees one, it folds the legs in and lets the robot down.

The supervisor also handles faults that are harder to show here: a lost radio link, a motor that dies, a frozen sensor. In simulation it handled every scenario I wrote for it.

8 · The real floor

What changes when the floor is real?

Everything on this page runs the robot's own program: the same estimator, controller, network and supervisor, compiled unchanged, using about 2 % of each 2 ms cycle. Here it walks on a floor that is exactly as the simulator says, with motors that never warm up and a body that matches its model to the gram.

A real floor is less polite. Friction changes from one step to the next, motors heat as they work, and the body is never quite what the model says. The network learned how hard to push in a world without those surprises. How much of that knowledge survives contact with the real thing is the question this work walks towards.

Other directions I find worth trying:

  • Learn forces on top of the tuned planner, to see whether they still add something.
  • Let the network choose footholds too, at least on easy ground where the simulator's derivatives can be trusted.
  • Teach the motors' heat limits during training. The knees run above their continuous rating with every controller.
  • Give it a way to see stairs before stepping on them.

The simulation is MuJoCo 3.7.0 and the robot's onboard C++ controller compiled to WebAssembly, with the trained networks and the test ground from the thesis. It differs from the robot build in two ways: it runs on one thread, and the robot is lifted clear of the ground before it starts.
